The DTV is built around remote work, but the rules about what counts as permitted work — and what doesn't — trip people up. Here's the honest, straightforward answer.

## What you can do on a DTV visa

### Work for a foreign employer (remotely)

Standard remote employment for a company based outside Thailand is exactly what the DTV is designed to allow.

### Freelance for foreign clients

Freelance or contract work for clients outside Thailand is permitted, provided the income is genuinely foreign-sourced.

### Run an online business registered abroad

If your business is registered outside Thailand and your customers/clients are outside Thailand too, operating it while living in Thailand on a DTV is permitted.

## What you cannot do on a DTV visa

### Work for a Thai company

Any employment relationship with a Thai company requires a work permit — the DTV doesn't cover this, regardless of whether the work is remote in nature.

### Freelance for Thai clients

Taking on Thai clients or Thai-sourced income shifts you outside what the DTV permits — this requires separate work authorisation.

### Teach, train, or instruct locally (without a work permit)

Even informal teaching or training delivered in Thailand — language tutoring, fitness instruction, workshops — counts as local work requiring a permit, regardless of how it's structured or paid.

## Summary: DTV work rules

The dividing line is straightforward in principle: foreign employer or foreign clients, foreign-sourced income — permitted. Thai employer, Thai clients, or work physically delivered to people in Thailand — not permitted without a work permit.

## Tax considerations for DTV holders

Spending 180 or more days in Thailand within a calendar year can establish Thai tax residency status, which has implications for how your income may be treated under Thai tax law — worth a conversation with a tax adviser if this applies to your situation.

## Can I get a work permit on a DTV?

Generally no — work permits are built around a Non-Immigrant B visa and Thai company sponsorship, a different visa category and process from the DTV. If your circumstances genuinely require Thai employment, transitioning to a Non-B visa and work permit is the correct path, not adding a work permit on top of a DTV.

## What "remote work" means on the DTV — the legal reality

The practical test isn't where you're physically sitting when you work — it's where your employer or clients are based, and where the income originates. Working from a Chiang Mai cafe for a US client is the DTV's exact use case; working from that same cafe for a Thai client is not.

## Tax implications for remote workers on a DTV

Beyond the 180-day residency threshold, some DTV holders also have tax obligations in their home country depending on their citizenship and tax residency rules there — the DTV doesn't change your home-country tax position, only your Thai one.

## DTV vs work permit: which is right for you?

If your income is genuinely foreign-sourced and your work doesn't involve Thai clients or a Thai employer, the DTV is the right, simpler tool. If you need to work for or be paid by a Thai entity, you need a Non-B visa and work permit instead — see our [business visa and work permit guide](/work-permits-business-visas-chiang-mai/) for that process.
